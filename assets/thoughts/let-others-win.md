In basketball, the scoreboard records points. Teams remember assists.

I played for my school team, and the passes I remember best are the ones that let someone else score. The ball left my hands, someone else's name went into the scorebook, and somehow it felt better than scoring myself. Years later, I think that feeling was the most useful thing I learned on that court.

## AI is good at tasks. Humans are needed for each other.

AI is taking over more and more of the visible work. It writes, codes, summarises and plans. So what's left for us?

The data points one way. The [WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025](https://www.weforum.org/publications/the-future-of-jobs-report-2025/in-full/3-skills-outlook/) lists **empathy and active listening** among the top core skills. [Half of the employers surveyed](https://www.fm-magazine.com/news/2025/feb/analytical-thinking-remains-top-core-skill-for-employers/) call it essential. When Indeed's Hiring Lab mapped how well GenAI can perform each skill, [empathy and active listening landed at the "very low capacity" end](https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/01/28/world-economic-forum-joint-research).

**Simple example:** an AI can draft a perfect "great job on the launch" message in two seconds. It can't notice that your designer stayed quiet in standup because her idea got cut, and then ask her for a coffee. The first is a task. The second is the job.

At BMSCE, I was on the core committee of our Rotaract Club and led several goodwill events. The biggest lesson wasn't about logistics. It was that **an event works when the volunteers feel it's theirs.**

## The research: givers finish first (and last)

Adam Grant's research in *Give and Take* found something surprising. [Givers end up at both the bottom *and* the top of the success ladder](https://www.leadershipnow.com/leadingblog/2013/04/are_you_a_giver_or_a_taker.html), with takers and matchers in the middle. What separates the two groups is that successful givers are "otherish". They [give more than they receive but keep their own interests in sight](https://www.leadershipnow.com/leadingblog/2013/04/are_you_a_giver_or_a_taker.html).

So "let others win" doesn't mean "let yourself lose". It means stop playing a zero-sum game.

The long-term payoff is even clearer. The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed people [since 1938](https://news.harvard.edu/Gazette/Story/2023/02/Work-Out-Daily-Ok-But-How-Socially-Fit-Are-You/) and concluded that [relationships are what give us the greatest happiness](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2023/08/relationships-basis-long-healthy-life/). The people [most satisfied in their relationships at 50 were the healthiest at 80](https://rosetta.to/u/ted/what-makes-a-good-life-lessons-from-the-longest-study-on-happiness-robert-waldin?t=0).

Letting others win is how you build those relationships. I've kept many friend groups for a long time, and I'm convinced none of them survived because I was keeping score.

## Three ways to practise it

1. **The assist.** Before you present, ask yourself whose work made this slide possible, and say their name out loud.
2. **The five-minute favour.** Grant's rule: [be willing to do anything that takes five minutes or less for anybody](https://www.leadershipnow.com/leadingblog/2013/04/are_you_a_giver_or_a_taker.html). An intro, a resume review, a quick explanation.
3. **The quiet listen.** In your next meeting, find the person who hasn't spoken and ask what they think. Then actually listen.

## The takeaway

In a world where AI makes everyone more productive, being productive stops setting you apart. What still sets you apart is being the person others want to win *with*.

The scoreboard will always record points. Make sure people remember your assists.
