Every MBA student has been in this group. Four people, one deck, a 9 a.m. deadline. One person rewrites everyone's slides at 2 a.m. Someone else goes quiet and lets the group carry them. The deck ships. Nobody is happy.

I think the fix is simple arithmetic.

## The arithmetic

A mean is the sum divided by the count. So if every number goes up, the mean has to go up. That is the whole idea.

Here is a four-person team, with scores out of 10:

| Scenario | Scores | Group mean |
|---|---|---|
| Today | 9, 5, 5, 5 | **6.0** |
| The star works harder (9 → 10) | 10, 5, 5, 5 | **6.25** |
| The star helps the others get to 7 | 9, 7, 7, 7 | **7.5** |

When the star pushes their own score higher, the mean moves by 0.25. When everyone gets better, it moves by 1.5. Improvement at the top gets harder and harder to come by, and the bottom is where most of the room is.

## The paradox

Here is the tension. **The goal is the group mean, not the individual best. But the only way to raise the group mean is for each person to do their individual best.**

It sounds like a contradiction. It isn't. Your individual best is how you get there, and the group mean is where you are going. Things go wrong when people mix up the two:

- **The Topper mindset** treats your own best as the goal. You hoard notes, take over discussions, and quietly hope a teammate slips. That is where the toxicity comes from.
- **The Passenger mindset** treats the group as the goal and forgets that it is made of individuals. "The team will handle it." Psychologists have a name for this: the [Ringelmann effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ringelmann_effect). The bigger the group pulling the rope, the less effort each person puts in.

Both lose. One burns the team out. The other drains it.

## What the research says

This isn't just a feel-good idea. In a [2010 study published in *Science*](https://www.gwern.net/doc/iq/2010-woolley.pdf), Anita Woolley and colleagues put 699 people into groups of two to five and found a measurable "collective intelligence" factor. That factor was [not strongly correlated with the average or the highest individual intelligence in the group](https://www.labourline.org/KENTIKA-19390415124911186979-Evidence-for-a-collective-inte.htm). It tracked how socially sensitive the members were and how evenly they took turns speaking. In fact, [groups where one person dominated were *less* collectively intelligent](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2010/09/100930143339.htm).

A later [meta-analysis](https://minerva-access.unimelb.edu.au/server/api/core/bitstreams/f8f21c2c-45d6-5bfb-ac3c-d4b116e1cf7c/content) found that the correlation between members' average IQ and group performance is only about **0.28**. Smart individuals don't automatically make a smart team.

Google found the same thing at scale. In Project Aristotle, [*who* is on a team mattered less than how the members interact and view their contributions](https://pagely.com/blog/google-team-building-process/), and psychological safety came out as [the strongest of the five dynamics](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness).

## Making it practical

Three rules I try to follow in every team, whether it's a case competition, a sprint, or a study group:

1. **Compete with your yesterday, not your teammate.** Your benchmark is your last draft, not the person sitting next to you.
2. **Your best includes making others better.** Explaining a model to a teammate counts as output. In the table above, it was the highest-return move.
3. **Say the mean out loud.** Start the meeting with "Where's the team weakest?", not "Who did the most?"

When our team reached the national semi-finals of The Ken's case competition (top 7% of 6,000+ teams), I couldn't point to one person's slide that got us there. Every section was solid. That's what a high mean looks like.

## The takeaway

Do your best **for** the mean, not **instead of** it. If everyone does that, the arithmetic takes care of the rest, and nobody has to be rewriting slides at 2 a.m.
